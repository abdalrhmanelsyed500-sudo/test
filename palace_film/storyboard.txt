================================================================================
PALACE  —  A Short Film
Written, directed and produced for procedural Blender-Python production
Total running time: exactly 300.0 seconds (15 shots)
================================================================================

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PHASE 1 — FIVE ORIGINAL CONCEPTS, EVALUATED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CONCEPT 1 — "The Cartographer of Lost Sounds"
  A sound recordist travels a coastal city cataloguing the sounds of things
  about to disappear (a telephone box, a bakery oven, a funicular) before they
  are gone; his recorder dies with one recording left to make.
  Originality .......... 9/10   Emotional impact ... 8/10
  Narrative complexity . 8/10   Visual potential ... 8/10
  Ending strength ...... 7/10   Feasibility ........ 4/10 (many locations)
  Verdict: Beautiful premise, but demands many distinct locations and heavy
  ambient audio design; a single-location piece will out-perform it on air.

CONCEPT 2 — "The Tideworker"
  In a drowned town revealed only at low tide, a man walks the seabed each
  dawn to scavenge what the sea returns, until one morning it returns
  something he recognizes.
  Originality .......... 8/10   Emotional impact ... 7/10
  Narrative complexity . 7/10   Visual potential ... 9/10 (strong silhhouettes)
  Ending strength ...... 6/10   Feasibility ........ 4/10 (large tidal sets)
  Verdict: Visually gorgeous but expensive to stage; ending drifts toward
  melancholy cliché. Rejected on production grounds.

CONCEPT 3 — "Signal"
  A night radio operator on a tidal island intercepts the same distress call
  every night for a year, and rows out to find its source.
  Originality .......... 7/10   Emotional impact ... 6/10
  Narrative complexity . 8/10   Visual potential ... 7/10
  Ending strength ...... 8/10   Feasibility ........ 5/10 (sea, boat, island,
  interior console — 4 sets plus VFX rain/waves)
  Verdict: Strong hook, but tension design needs sound-mix sophistication that
  a CPU-rendered 5-minute piece cannot fully honor; leans thriller over heart.

CONCEPT 4 — "The Migrators"
  In a high-rise where floor numbers have been erased, an elevator operator
  rides to floors that no longer exist, carrying parcels addressed to people
  who moved away years ago.
  Originality .......... 9/10   Emotional impact ... 7/10
  Narrative complexity . 9/10   Visual potential ... 7/10
  Ending strength ...... 7/10   Feasibility ........ 5/10 (vertical city set,
  multi-floor continuity)
  Verdict: Inventive, but its emotional logic is opaque without dialogue;
  too abstract for the target viewer in 5 minutes.

CONCEPT 5 — "PALACE" (SELECTED)
  On the night his failing seaside cinema screens its final show to an empty
  house, an aging projectionist discovers who has been sitting in Row F all
  along — and passes on the one thing he has left.
  Originality .......... 8/10   Emotional impact ... 9/10
  Narrative complexity . 8/10   Visual potential ... 10/10 (beam of light,
  dust, velvet seats, neon sign, rain, dawn)
  Ending strength ...... 9/10   Feasibility ........ 9/10 (ONE location,
  two characters, no dialogue, all procedural)
  Verdict: One location, two characters, a wordless story told in light.
  Selected.

WHY IT WINS: the cinema is the perfect bottle for visual storytelling —
light literally is the medium (projection). The empty house / one small
viewer arc is told through composition, color and sound rather than dialogue.
The paper-boat motif plants, pays off and resolves. It avoids every forbidden
trope: no death reveals, no letters, no time travel (the 1953 beat is a
memory, not a plot device), no lost child rescue plot (the boy is not in
danger; he is simply the last audience).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PHASE 2 — LOGLINE, THEME, MOTIFS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

LOGLINE
  On the night his failing seaside cinema screens its final show to an empty
  house, an aging projectionist discovers his one loyal viewer has been living
  above the theater all along — and decides what, and who, the last show is
  really for.

THEME
  Light is a form of care. A public ritual performed faithfully for strangers
  (and one secret child) is never wasted, even if nobody buys a ticket.
  Cinema survives not as a building but as a seat that gets sat in.

MOTIFS (planted -> paid off)
  1. PAPER BOATS. Elias used to fold a program into a boat for every premiere.
     Act 1: he finds a child's small boat on Row F, center. He assumes a
     litterbug. Act 4: the boy folds the Farewell handbill into the same
     boat-shape and leaves it on F7. Elias realizes the “litter” was a message
     — and this time it is one he is meant to receive.
  2. ROW F, SEAT 7. Composed dead-center in three separate shots; never named
     in dialogue (there is none). The final shot lands sunlight on it.
  3. THE LIT ATTIC WINDOW. Shot 1 (exterior, dawn): one window above the
     Palace glows warm while the sign is dead. Recalled in Shot 10 when the
     boy slips in. Recalled once more in the final exterior. It was him,
     every night, watching the beam through the floorboards.
  4. THE PROJECTIONIST'S HANDS. Close-ups of thread, oil, the big lever.
     Craft as devotion.
  5. COLD/WARM. Exterior = cold teal; interior house = cold grey until the
     beam is lit; beam + boy's raincoat + screen = the only warm/saturated
     notes in the film. Color literally follows feeling.
  6. RAIN. Starts as drizzle in Act 3, full storm in Act 4; during the show
     it falls silent (mix duck), so the film-within-the-film sound design sits
     forward — the boy's world narrows to the screen.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PHASE 3 — SCREENPLAY (wordless; intertitles only at the very end)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

FADE IN:

EXT. SEASIDE PROMENADE — DAWN
A shuttered resort. Gulls. A boarded arcade. The PALACE CINEMA marquee hangs
dead at the end of the street. One attic window above the facade glows faint
amber. A hand-painted banner: "TONIGHT — FINAL SCREENING. THANK YOU FOR
61 YEARS."

ELIAS (60s), forearm brace of keys on a canvas satchel, bicycle clips, walks
the empty promenade toward the cinema, thermos in one hand, a paper bag of
pull-up letter tiles under the other arm. He does not hurry. He never
hurries.

INT. BOX OFFICE / LOBBY — MORNING
Keys. The lock fights him, then gives. The door exhales dust and cold air.
He steps into the dark lobby. Velvet shapes wait in the auditorium beyond the
doors like sleeping animals. He touches the brass ticket lamp — dead — and
sets down the OPEN sign anyway, at the counter, where nobody will see it.

INT. PROJECTION BOOTH — MORNING
He climbs. The booth is a chapel: two projectors ("The Empress" — the live
one), reels like metal halos, an oil can, a stool, and on the wall a 1953
photograph: the young PALACE ablaze with bulbs, a boy in shorts held by the
hand in front of it. Elias polishes The Empress with a rag. His reflection
swims across the porthole glass, over the empty seats below.

INT. AUDITORIUM — LATE MORNING
He sweeps rows with a wide broom. Midway down Row F: a SMALL PAPER BOAT,
folded from a bus ticket, nudged against a seat leg. He crouches. Turns it
over. Nothing written. He looks up the raked rows toward the lit attic hatch
in the ceiling. Says nothing. Pockets the boat. As he leaves: ECU, his shoe
uncovers a pattern in the dust — SMALL BARE FOOTPRINTS beneath the seat. He
stops. Looks at the booth ladder. (The audience was overhead.)

INT. BOX OFFICE — AFTERNOON
He inks the date stamp — FINAL. He opens the ledger: the advance bookings
page for tonight is blank. Every page for weeks is blank. He tears yesterday's
blank page out of habit, then laughs once, quietly, at himself. He tears it
into strips, small and precise, and folds them into one neat pile.
(He cannot bring himself to fold THESE into a boat.)
Clock ticks. First drops of rain stipple the high lobby windows.

EXT. MARQUEE — LATE AFTERNOON
On a ladder in rising wind, he swaps the last letters by hand:
T O N I G H T   O N L Y . Thunder leans across the bay. He climbs down with
the old "CLOSED" letters stacked like shingles, and does not look back at
the attic window. (He knows it is lit. He has known for weeks.)

INT. PROJECTION BOOTH — EVENING
He threads tonight's film: an ancient, hand-tinted print, THE SEA AND PAPA,
because it was the first film the Palace ever showed in 1953. He checks the
lamp. Mains THUNK. The motor sighs awake. Through the porthole: 800 empty
seats. His ghost-face hangs in the dark glass over them. He sinks onto the
stool. On the wall clock: 7:28.

Nobody comes. The clock is the only audience. Rain needles the roof. He puts
his hand on the big changeover lever — the ritual matters even if nobody
sees it. He pulls. The lamp dies to standby. The house goes black. 7:30.
The last show of the Palace.

INT. LOBBY — NIGHT
The stage/side door bangs open on a gust. A BOY (9), soaked to a second
skin, yellow raincoat glowing in emergency lamp-light, breathes in ragged
gusts. In his fist: a crumpled BUS TICKET — one coin's worth — and the
farewell banner handbill he has peeled and folded small. He sees Elias. He
is not ashamed. He is ready: he holds the coin out on a flat palm.

Elias looks at the coin. At the boy. At the locked auditorium doors. He
takes a wax-pencil stub and, on the back of a cardboard ticket roll, writes
SEAT F7 — and tears it free as an ADMIT ONE stub. He presses it into the
boy's palm, curls the boy's fingers over it, and — for the first time all
day — something in his face unknots. He opens the house doors.

INT. AUDITORIUM — NIGHT
He dims the houselights. The motor takes. The beam CUTS the dark — a solid
amber blade full of dust — and lands on the screen. The first frame
blooms: a painted sea, waves rolling in watercolor time. The boy at F7
does not sit so much as arrive, planted, boots up on nothing, coat dripping
an arc of rain-water onto Row F carpet.

INT. AUDITORIUM / MEMORY — NIGHT (INTERCUT)
Close on the boy's face — rain-streaked, held open, lit by the living
picture. Close on Elias's eye at the booth porthole, glasses catching the
picture-light.

MEMORY — 1953 (SEPIA): the original opening night. A younger projectionist
— ELIAS'S FATHER — hands small Elias up so he can see over the balustrade. A
lamp-lit marquee: NOW SHOWING — THE SEA AND PAPA. Little Elias, held by two
warm hands, gasps as the beam ignites over a thousand hats and heads. The
camera pushes in on his face, which is the same face as the boy in F7.
MATCH CUT on the eyes.

BACK TO NOW: the boy's feet swing, not reaching the floor. The paper boat
(the one Elias pocketed) sits on the armrest beside him where Elias,
wordless, has placed it earlier — wait. Clarity: Elias set the found boat
on F7's armrest before the show. The boy's eyes flick to it. Reverent. He
knows it means him.

INT. AUDITORIUM — NIGHT (END OF REEL)
The final frame holds. The leader flaps, flaps, flaps — white. The motor
spins down with a long sigh. Dark. Rain, suddenly audible again, very far
away. The boy does not move. Then the CHAIR SQUEAK. He is standing at F7.
On the seat: the farewell handbill, now folded — with quick, practiced,
precise hands — into a perfect SMALL PAPER BOAT, the same fold Elias
invented. He looks up toward the booth. Raises the boat one inch. Puts it
down tenderly on F7's armrest. Pulls up his hood. Leaves the way he came,
door swinging.

INT. BOOTH / AUDITORIUM — DEEP NIGHT
Elias comes down with the work-light. Stops at F7. Reads the boat. Picks it
up like it weighs something. Walks it up to the booth, sets it beneath the
1953 photograph, next to The Empress. He does the shutdown ritual — lamp,
motor, breakers — one by one, hands steady. Beside the door he hangs the
keys on the old hook labeled simply OPEN. Click.

INT. AUDITORIUM — DAWN (NEXT MORNING)
Sunrise through the lobby glass finds the auditorium in shafts. Dust motes.
800 empty seats. Row F — one seat occupied by a single ARCH of
sunlight carrying, for a few seconds, the white paper boat on its armrest.
Then cloud, and the shaft slides off and is gone.

EXT. PALACE CINEMA — MORNING
Wide, slow dolly-out: the dead marquee, the sea beyond, gulls. One attic
window, shuttered for the last time. On the doors, a hand-taped note, in
Elias's careful block letters: "THANK YOU FOR 61 YEARS — THE MANAGEMENT."
He stands small on the pavement with his satchel. He does not lock the
doors — there is nothing left to lock. He walks toward the camera, passes
out of frame left. Hold on the building.

CUT TO BLACK.

A single projector CLICK in the dark.

TITLE CARD (hand-lettered cream on black, fades up):
    PALACE
    for everyone who ever had a favorite seat

END.

PHASE 5 — ART DIRECTION / PALETTE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

STYLE: stylized low-poly / miniature diorama, flat-shaded, Cycles CPU.
No photorealism; every surface intentional. Camera lenses 35–50mm feel.

PALETTE (hex)
  exterior pre-dawn teal ... #1B2E33    sky low ............ #27424A
  sea ..................... #123840    wet promenade ....... #2B3A3E
  cinema facade cream ..... #8F8574    marquee dead ........ #4A4239
  interior near-black ..... #14100D    velvet red (desat) .. #4F2121
  wood dark ............... #33241A    brass / copper ...... #B0803F
  projector body .......... #2A2724    paper white ......... #F2EDE3
  boy's raincoat YELLOW ... #E8B53A    glow warm ........... #F5D9A0
  1953 sepia .............. #C9A25E on #6B4F2C

Color arc: cold world -> first warm note = attic window (S01) -> full warm =
beam ignition (S11) -> maximum warmth in S12 -> after shutdown, one shaft of
sunrise pried through cloud holds the boat white-hot (S15) before the letdown.

CHARACTER SILHOUETTES
  ELIAS — long cardigan to the knees, flat cap, narrow shoulders, forearms
  always slightly bent (a lifter's habit from reels), glasses that catch
  light. Walk: 4/4 deliberate, heel-first.
  THE BOY — oversized hooded raincoat (too big, hand-me-down), short legs,
  swings his feet always, cradles the boat to his chest when running.
